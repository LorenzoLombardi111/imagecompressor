Why does image optimization matter for websites?

Page speed directly affects SEO. Google uses Core Web Vitals as ranking signals, and the most image-dependent metric is Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) — the time it takes for the largest visible element to render. On most pages, the largest element is an image. A 3MB hero image that takes 4 seconds to load on a mobile connection will push your LCP into the "poor" range, directly hurting your Google ranking.

Visitors leave slow pages. Research consistently shows that 53% of mobile visitors abandon a page that takes more than 3 seconds to load. Every additional second of load time increases bounce rate. For e-commerce sites, slow image loading directly reduces conversion rates — Amazon famously found that every 100ms of latency cost them 1% in sales.

Bandwidth costs money. If your site serves 100,000 page views per month and each page loads 5MB of images, that is 500GB of image bandwidth. Compressing those images to 1.5MB each reduces bandwidth to 150GB — a 70% reduction that translates directly to lower hosting costs.

Mobile users have data limits. Many of your visitors are on metered mobile connections. Serving oversized images consumes their data allowance unnecessarily. Optimized images are a basic courtesy to your audience.

What image format should you use for websites?

Quick format guide: WebP for everything if possible · JPG for photos when WebP is not supported · PNG only for graphics with transparency that need pixel accuracy · GIF only for simple animations

WebP is the best general-purpose web format. WebP produces files 25–35% smaller than JPG and PNG at equivalent quality, supports both lossy and lossless compression, and handles transparency. All modern browsers support WebP — Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge. Unless you need to support very old browsers, WebP should be your default. freeimagecompressor.app can convert any image to WebP with one click.

JPG for photographs. If WebP is not an option, JPG is the standard format for photographic images. At 80% quality, JPG provides excellent compression for photos with millions of colors. JPG does not support transparency.

PNG for graphics with transparency. Use PNG for logos, icons, and graphics that need transparent backgrounds and pixel-perfect edges. PNG files are larger than JPG or WebP equivalents, so only use PNG when you specifically need its lossless quality or transparency and WebP is not available.

What dimensions should website images be?

Serving an image larger than its display size wastes bandwidth. A common mistake is uploading a 4000×3000 phone photo to a blog where it is displayed at 800px wide — the browser downloads 10x more pixels than it shows. freeimagecompressor.app's resize option helps you right-size images before uploading.

  • Hero/banner images: 1920px wide maximum. This covers full-width layouts on large monitors. For sites that max out at a narrower content width, use that width instead.
  • Content/blog images: 800–1200px wide. Most blog content areas are 600–800px wide, so 800–1200px provides enough resolution for high-DPI (Retina) displays without excess.
  • Thumbnails and cards: 300–400px wide. For image grids, related post thumbnails, and card layouts.
  • Social media previews (OG images): 1200×630px. This is the standard Open Graph image size that displays well across Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, and other platforms.

How do you optimize images for WordPress?

WordPress sites are particularly prone to slow image loading because content editors often upload photos directly from phones or cameras without any optimization. While WordPress plugins like ShortPixel, Imagify, and Smush can handle server-side compression, they either cost money for significant usage or consume your server's CPU resources.

A simpler approach is to optimize images before uploading them to WordPress. Use freeimagecompressor.app to batch compress your blog images: set quality to 80%, choose WebP format (if your theme supports it), resize to a maximum width of 1200px for content images, and download the ZIP. Then upload the optimized images to your WordPress media library. This approach works with any WordPress hosting plan, requires no plugins, and gives you full control over quality settings.

For WordPress sites that need WebP but have older themes, you can upload both JPG and WebP versions and use a simple plugin to serve WebP to browsers that support it. However, with all modern browsers now supporting WebP, this is increasingly unnecessary.
