Why are PNG files so large?

PNG stands for Portable Network Graphics, and it uses lossless compression. This means every single pixel in the image is preserved exactly — no visual information is ever discarded. This is the fundamental reason PNGs are large: preserving all the data requires more storage space.

Compare this to JPG, which throws away visual data that human eyes are unlikely to notice. A 4000×3000 photograph saved as PNG might be 15MB, while the same image saved as JPG at 80% quality could be 1.5MB — a 10x reduction — with barely any visible difference.

PNG makes sense for specific use cases: screenshots with crisp text, logos and icons, graphics with transparency, and any image where pixel-perfect accuracy matters. For photographs and complex images with millions of colors, PNG is almost always the wrong format.

How does browser-based PNG compression work?

When you compress a PNG in freeimagecompressor.app, the browser reads the image, draws it to an HTML5 Canvas, and re-encodes it as PNG using canvas.toBlob('image/png'). The Canvas API's PNG encoder uses the deflate compression algorithm — the same algorithm PNG always uses — but the specific encoding choices (filter selection, compression level) may differ from the original file's encoding.

This means the re-encoded PNG is sometimes slightly smaller than the original (if the original was not optimally encoded) and sometimes slightly larger. The quality parameter that works well for JPG and WebP has limited or no effect on PNG output in most browsers, because PNG compression is lossless by definition — you cannot discard quality to save space.

Honest assessment: For PNG-to-PNG compression, freeimagecompressor.app typically achieves 5–15% size reduction. For dramatically smaller files, convert to WebP (25–35% smaller) or JPG (50–80% smaller, if you don't need transparency).

When should you convert PNG to another format?

Convert PNG to JPG when: The image is a photograph or complex illustration with no transparency. JPG files are typically 50–80% smaller than the equivalent PNG. The lossy compression introduces minor artifacts, but at 80% quality these are imperceptible for most images. Use this for blog photos, product images, and background graphics.

Convert PNG to WebP when: You want the best compression while optionally preserving transparency. WebP supports both lossy and lossless compression and typically produces files 25–35% smaller than PNG. All modern browsers support WebP. This is often the best choice — smaller than both PNG and JPG, with transparency support.

Keep PNG when: You need pixel-perfect accuracy for screenshots with text, logos, icons, or any image where sharp edges and exact colors matter. Keep PNG when transparency is required and WebP is not an option for your audience. Keep PNG for technical diagrams, UI mockups, and anything with crisp lines that JPG would blur.

How does freeimagecompressor.app handle PNG transparency?

When you keep the output format as PNG, the Canvas API preserves the alpha channel (transparency). Your transparent PNGs will remain transparent after compression. However, if you convert a PNG to JPG, transparency is lost — the JPG format does not support alpha channels, and transparent areas will be rendered as white.

Converting to WebP preserves transparency. This makes WebP an excellent alternative to PNG when you need both small file sizes and transparency support — it gives you the best of both worlds.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can PNGs be compressed without losing quality?

Yes, but with limited results. PNG is a lossless format, so lossless compression tools can reduce file size somewhat by optimizing the encoding without discarding any pixel data. However, the gains are typically modest (5-20%). For dramatic size reduction, you need lossy PNG compression (like TinyPNG's pngquant approach) or conversion to JPG/WebP.

Should I convert PNG to JPG?

It depends. Convert to JPG if your image is a photograph or does not require transparency — JPG files are typically 50-80% smaller than equivalent PNGs. Keep PNG if you need transparency (logos, icons, overlays) or if the image has sharp text/lines that would suffer from JPG compression artifacts.

PNG is designed for images where exact pixel reproduction matters — screenshots, graphics with text, logos, and images requiring transparency.
